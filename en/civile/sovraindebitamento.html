Debts are not a life
sentence. The law provides a way out.

Mortgage instalments, finance companies, tax debts, attachments (pignoramenti): when debts exceed repayment capacity, the Italian legal system provides tangible instruments to restructure debt exposure, halt enforcement actions, and in certain cases obtain the full cancellation of debts. The Business Crisis and Insolvency Code (Codice della Crisi d’Impresa e dell’Insolvenza) has fundamentally reformed these protections. We have an in-depth understanding of them.

Regulatory framework

The Crisis Code has changed everything

Please note: with the entry into force of D.Lgs. 14/2019 (CCII), over-indebtedness proceedings already initiated under Law 3/2012 remain effective, but new petitions follow the regime of the Crisis Code. The distinction is significant for procedural time limits, requirements of merit (meritevolezza), and the consequences of judicial confirmation (omologazione).
Main reference provision

D.Lgs. 14/2019 (CCII) — Business Crisis and Insolvency Code

The Crisis Code, which came into force on 15 July 2022, entirely reformed the rules on insolvency for non-bankruptable entities: consumers, small businesses, professionals, and agricultural entrepreneurs. Over-indebtedness proceedings, previously governed by L. 3/2012 (the so-called “anti-suicide” law), are now governed by artt. 65-83 CCII (restructuring of consumer debts), artt. 74-83 (minor arrangement - concordato minore for other entities), and artt. 268-283 (controlled liquidation). Art. 282 CCII introduces the debt discharge of the penniless debtor (esdebitazione del debitore incapiente) — the so-called fresh start — which allows for the cancellation of all debts even in the absence of any assets. The common prerequisite is merit (meritevolezza): the debtor must not have caused their insolvency through intent or gross negligence.

Effective from 15 July 2022 — repeals L. 3/2012 for new petitions (ricorsi)

The role of the OCC (Crisis Settlement Body - Organismo di Composizione della Crisi) is central: every over-indebtedness proceeding must be handled with the support of an OCC registered in the relevant register. The OCC appoints a crisis manager who assists the debtor in drafting the plan, verifies merit (meritevolezza), and liaises with creditors and the Court. The choice of the OCC and the quality of the manager’s report have a decisive impact on the outcome of the proceeding.

The restructuring of consumer debts (artt. 65-73 CCII) differs from the minor arrangement (concordato minore) because it does not require creditor consent: the plan is assessed and confirmed (omologato) by the Court on the basis of its advantage compared to the liquidation alternative and the debtor’s merit (meritevolezza). This makes the procedure particularly effective in cases where creditors do not cooperate.

Available instruments

The CCII procedures

The CCII provides different instruments for different situations. Choosing the most suitable procedure depends on the nature of the debts, the presence or absence of liquidable assets, and the legal status of the debtor.

📋

Restructuring of Consumer Debts

Artt. 65-73 CCII. For consumer debtors acting for purposes unrelated to business activity. The plan is confirmed (omologato) by the Court without requiring a vote by creditors. It requires merit (meritevolezza) and economic convenience for creditors compared to liquidation.

📈

Consumer Plan

An operational instrument within debt restructuring: the debtor proposes a multi-year repayment plan, including partial payment, that takes into account available income and assets. It may provide for a reduction of principal and interest. Confirmed (omologato) by the Court upon proposal by the OCC.

🤝

Minor Arrangement (Concordato Minore)

Artt. 74-83 CCII. For entities other than consumers (small entrepreneurs, professionals, start-ups). It requires an affirmative vote by the majority of creditors by value. It allows the business activity to continue. Flexible in the structure of the plan.

⚖

Controlled Liquidation (Liquidazione Controllata)

Artt. 268-277 CCII. An alternative to bankruptcy liquidation for non-bankruptable entities. The debtor’s assets are entrusted to a liquidator appointed by the Court, who sells them to satisfy creditors. Upon completion, the debtor may petition for debt discharge (esdebitazione) regarding residual liabilities.

🌟

Discharge for the Penniless Debtor (Esdebitazione Incapiente)

Art. 282 CCII. A debtor who has no assets to liquidate and cannot propose any repayment plan may obtain the full cancellation of all past debts (fresh start). Subject to the requirement of merit (meritevolezza) and the absence of acts committed in fraud of creditors over the preceding five years.

🔒

Crisis Composition Agreement (Accordo di Composizione Crisi)

A negotiated instrument (L. 3/2012 for pending proceedings; minor arrangement - concordato minore under the new CCII) that allows reaching an agreement with creditors representing at least 60% of claims, with judicial confirmation (omologazione) and binding effects also on dissenting creditors. It presupposes active cooperation with primary creditors.

Our approach

Our strategies

Every path out of over-indebtedness requires a thorough analysis of the debtor’s assets, income, and family situation. There is no standard path: we formulate the solution on a case-by-case basis.

01

Assessment of Merit (Meritevolezza)

The first step is to verify whether the debtor meets the merit requirement (requisito di meritevolezza) provided for by the CCII (Codice della crisi d'impresa e dell'insolvenza): the insolvency must not have been caused by fraud, willful misconduct, or gross negligence. We analyze the debt history, the reasons for the indebtedness, and the conduct of the debtor to build a solid profile capable of withstanding the scrutiny of the Court (Tribunale) and the OCC (Organismo di Composizione della Crisi).

02

Restructuring Plan

We prepare a sustainable restructuring plan based on disposable income, necessary expenses for family maintenance, and future income prospects. The plan must be more advantageous for creditors compared to the liquidation alternative, yet realistically feasible for the debtor in the medium term. The quality of the plan is the decisive factor for confirmation (omologazione).

03

Controlled Liquidation (Liquidazione Controllata)

When assets can be liquidated but debts vastly exceed the value of the assets, controlled liquidation (liquidazione controllata) allows for the orderly winding down of the debt position under court supervision. At the end of the procedure, the debtor may access discharge of residual debts (esdebitazione), obtaining a complete clearance of their position.

04

Debt Discharge — Fresh Start

The debt discharge of the penniless debtor (esdebitazione del debitore incapiente, art. 282 CCII) is the most radical instrument: it allows for the cancellation of all prior debts for those who have no liquidatable assets. We assist the client throughout the entire procedure: from the filing of the petition (ricorso) with the OCC, to the report of the crisis manager (gestore della crisi), up to the discharge decree issued by the Court (Tribunale).

05

OCC and Crisis Manager

The choice of the OCC and establishing the relationship with the crisis manager (gestore della crisi) are delicate phases that influence the outcome of the procedure. We operate in coordination with accredited OCCs, ensuring that the manager's report supports our client's position and that the procedure is carried out as expeditiously as possible.

06

Stay of Enforcement Proceedings

The filing of the petition for over-indebtedness triggers an automatic stay of individual enforcement proceedings: real estate foreclosures, administrative vehicle impounds (fermi amministrativi), and debt collection procedures. We act urgently to obtain the stay and grant relief to the debtor, protecting the family home and essential assets during the course of the proceedings.

Case Law and Scenarios

Five resolved cases

Over-indebtedness does not have a single face. These cases illustrate how diverse legal instruments, properly applied, produce tangible outcomes for individuals in widely varying situations.

Case Study — 01

Over-indebted family burdened by mortgage and consumer credit

Consumer debt restructuring pursuant to arts. 65-73 CCII — Naples — Total debt: 187,000 euros

A married couple with three dependent children found themselves in a state of progressive insolvency: a mortgage on their primary home (monthly installment of 1,100 euros), six financing agreements entered into with consumer credit lenders to cover medical and educational expenses, and tax liabilities for unpaid VAT and social security contributions. The family income stood at 2,800 euros per month, insufficient to service the total debt load of approximately 187,000 euros. Two wage or bank account garnishments (pignoramenti) were underway alongside the threat of mortgage foreclosure proceedings.

The preliminary assessment confirmed the requirement of meritoriousness: debts had been incurred for documentable family necessities, with no element of fraud or speculation. We filed an application (ricorso) for consumer debt restructuring with the assistance of an accredited crisis management body (OCC), simultaneously seeking the immediate stay of enforcement proceedings. The proposed plan provided for the payment of 35% of unsecured debts over 60 monthly installments, preserving the mortgage under unchanged conditions to safeguard the family home. The Court approved (omologato) the plan, holding the proposal to be more advantageous for creditors than the liquidation alternative, which would have yielded less given the absence of significant assets beyond the mortgaged property. The family was able to retain their home and embark on a structured path out of over-indebtedness.

✓ Plan approved — home preserved — enforcement actions stayed
Case Study — 02

Former business owner with unsustainable tax debts

Minor concordat (concordato minore) pursuant to arts. 74-83 CCII — Caserta — Tax debts: 320,000 euros

A former sole proprietor had shut down his business following a downturn in the construction sector, leaving outstanding liabilities with the Italian Revenue Agency (Agenzia delle Entrate) and the National Social Security Institute (INPS) totaling 320,000 euros, partly stemming from tax payment notices (cartelle esattoriali) already registered in the tax rolls. In the meantime, he had found employment as an employee with a net monthly salary of approximately 1,600 euros. Ordinary bankruptcy proceedings were not applicable because the business did not meet the statutory size thresholds under the Bankruptcy Law (L.F.) and could not access ordinary insolvency proceedings. The tax collection agency (Agenzia delle Entrate-Riscossione) had already registered a mortgage lien on a small apartment owned by the client.

His status as a ceased small business owner opened the path to the minor arrangement (concordato minore) under arts. 74-83 CCII, rather than consumer debt restructuring. Following a thorough review of the tax position, including the identification of time-barred or invalid tax demands, we proposed a minor concordat with payment of 20% of unsecured claims over 72 months, reserving for the secured creditor (the tax authority) the estimated liquidation value of the mortgaged asset. The tax collection agency (Agenzia delle Entrate-Riscossione), which held nearly all the claims, approved the plan. The Court sanctioned (omologato) the concordat, finding the proposal more favorable than liquidation. The client was able to retain the property and secured a structured six-year debt resolution plan.

✓ Minor concordat approved — debt reduced by 80%
Case Study — 03

Indigent debtor: total debt discharge

Discharge of debt for indigent debtor (esdebitazione incapiente) pursuant to art. 282 CCII — Naples — Cancelled debts: 94,000 euros

A 52-year-old precarious worker was burdened with personal debts amounting to 94,000 euros, accumulated over years of interrupted fixed-term contracts, periods of unemployment, and medical expenses for a non-self-sufficient family member. He owned no real estate, registered movable property of value, or savings. His only source of income was a part-time employment contract with a monthly salary of approximately 900 euros net, barely sufficient for basic subsistence. None of the traditional over-indebtedness procedures (procedure di sovraindebitamento) was feasible: there were no assets to liquidate, nor sufficient income for a credible payment plan.

The solution was the discharge of debts for the non-asset debtor (esdebitazione del debitore incapiente) pursuant to art. 282 CCII, a measure introduced specifically for these borderline cases. We meticulously documented the client’s worthiness (meritevolezza) — absence of fraudulent acts, indebtedness due to force majeure, total good faith — and filed the petition (ricorso) with the Crisis Settlement Body (OCC – Organismo di Composizione della Crisi) together with a report by the crisis manager certifying the objective impossibility of any plan. The Court (Tribunale), having verified the completeness of the documentation and the worthiness of the debtor, issued the discharge decree (decreto di esdebitazione) entirely cancelling all 94,000 euros of debt. Our client was able to make a fresh start, with full legal capacity to enter into new obligations.

✓ Total discharge of debts — 94,000 euros of debt cancelled
Case Study — 04

Consumer plan approved despite creditor dissent

Debt restructuring pursuant to CCII — Napoli — Debts: 210,000 euros

A 68-year-old pensioner had debts of 210,000 euros owed to five credit institutions and financial companies, contracted mainly through personal loans and salary-backed/pension-backed loans (cessione del quinto della pensione). Her net monthly pension was 1,450 euros, of which approximately 900 euros were committed to repayment instalments. Three credit institutions had declared themselves opposed to any out-of-court settlement and had threatened enforcement proceedings (procedure esecutive) against the home (co-owned with her children). The creditors contended that the proposed plan was insufficient.

The power of the consumer debt restructuring pursuant to the CCII compared to pre-reform procedures lies precisely in the irrelevance of the creditors’ vote: the Court (Tribunale) approves (omologa) the plan if it is worthy and more advantageous compared to the liquidation alternative, regardless of the creditors’ consent. We filed a plan providing for the payment of 28% of the unsecured claims (crediti chirografari) in 60 monthly instalments, supported by an expert valuation report demonstrating that the liquidation of the assets would have yielded less than 10% of total claims. The Court approved the plan notwithstanding the creditors’ opposition, deeming the proposal clearly more advantageous than the liquidation alternative. The family home was not affected by the enforcement proceedings.

✓ Plan approved despite dissent — home protected
Case Study — 05

Controlled liquidation with final fresh start

Controlled liquidation pursuant to arts. 268-277 CCII + discharge of debts (esdebitazione) — Napoli — Residual debts cancelled

A retail merchant had ceased business at a loss, leaving debts owed to suppliers, banks, and the tax authorities for over 480,000 euros. He owned commercial premises (estimated value 120,000 euros) and some furnishings and equipment of modest value. He was not subject to bankruptcy (fallimento) because the business was below the statutory size thresholds, but he had liquidatable assets that prevented direct access to the debt discharge for non-asset debtors (esdebitazione incapiente). The situation required a procedure that would convert the assets into liquidity for the creditors and pave the way for discharge from the residual debts.

The controlled liquidation (liquidazione controllata) pursuant to arts. 268-277 CCII was the appropriate legal tool: the liquidator appointed by the Court sold the commercial premises (proceeds of 115,000 euros) and the other assets, distributing the proceeds to creditors in accordance with the statutory ranking of priorities (criteri di prelazione) under the CCII. At the close of the proceedings, the unpaid residual debts amounted to approximately 365,000 euros — 85% of the total — which were entirely wiped out by a discharge decree (decreto di esdebitazione) pursuant to art. 278 CCII. Our client, after approximately twenty-four months of proceedings, obtained full release from all prior debts and was able to resume employment without the burden of unsustainable debt.

✓ Liquidation completed + fresh start: 365,000 euros of debt wiped out
