Has your application for special protection been rejected?
The right to stay can be defended. Forced return must be prevented.

Special protection is the form of humanitarian protection provided for by art. 19 of the Consolidated Immigration Act (Testo Unico sull’Immigrazione - TUI) for foreign nationals who, while not meeting the requirements for refugee status or subsidiary protection, cannot be expelled or returned to a country where they would face inhuman or degrading treatment, or where their right to private and family life would be violated. D.L. 130/2020 had significantly expanded this legal remedy, recognizing the right to remain based on social integration. Subsequent D.L. 20/2023 curtailed its scope, giving rise to case law disputes that are still developing. Our firm closely monitors legislative and judicial developments to formulate the most effective defense strategies under the current regulatory framework.

Regulatory framework

Special protection: from art. 19 TUI to the case law of the United Sections

Art. 19 par. 1 D.Lgs. 286/1998 (TUI) — Absolute non-refoulement

The prohibition of expulsion and return (respingimento) on grounds of risk of persecution or inhuman treatment

Art. 19, paragraph 1, of the Consolidated Immigration Act (Testo Unico sull’Immigrazione) establishes the absolute prohibition of expulsion or return (respingimento) of a foreign national to a country where they may be subjected to persecution on grounds of race, sex, language, nationality, religion, political opinions, personal or social conditions, or where they may face the risk of being sent onward to another State in which they would not be protected from persecution. This provision transposes the principle of non-refoulement under the 1951 Geneva Convention and art. 3 ECHR as interpreted by the European Court of Human Rights. The prohibition is absolute and non-derogable: it allows no exceptions, even for reasons of public order or national security. Paragraph 1.1, introduced by D.L. 130/2020 and subsequently reformulated by D.L. 20/2023, regulated protection in cases where expulsion would entail a violation of the right to private and family life, structuring the requirements differently from the prior version.

Art. 19 par. 1 TUI — D.Lgs. 286/1998
Art. 19 par. 1.1 TUI — D.L. 130/2020 and D.L. 20/2023

The evolution of protection for private and family life: a comparison between the two decrees

D.L. 21 October 2020, n. 130 (converted with amendments by Law 18 December 2020, n. 173) reformulated art. 19 par. 1.1 TUI, establishing a prohibition of expulsion whenever it would breach the individual's right to respect for private and family life, assessed taking into account the nature and effectiveness of family ties, social integration in Italy, length of stay, and the absence of ties to the country of origin. The rule thus codified broad protection anchored in the criteria developed by the European Court of Human Rights in interpreting art. 8 ECHR. Subsequent D.L. 10 March 2023, n. 20 (converted with amendments by Law 5 May 2023, n. 50, so-called «Cutro decree») amended the provision once more, narrowing protection for private and family life and abolishing protection for social integration as an autonomous ground for relief. However, lower courts have largely continued to apply protection directly under international conventions pursuant to art. 8 ECHR, holding that treaty law prevails over restrictive domestic legislation.

Art. 19 par. 1.1 TUI — D.L. 130/2020, D.L. 20/2023
Cass. SS.UU. n. 24413/2021 — The landmark ruling

The United Sections on the nature of special protection and the role of the court

The United Sections of the Court of Cassation (Corte di Cassazione), with judgment of 13 October 2021, n. 24413, defined the legal nature of special protection and the assessment criteria that the court must apply. The judgment — fundamental for anyone dealing with international protection — established that special protection is a legal remedy of a different nature from refugee status and subsidiary protection: it requires neither individual persecution nor the risk of serious harm, but is grounded in a comprehensive assessment of the applicant's situation, including their degree of integration within the Italian context and the situation in the country of origin. The United Sections clarified that the judge must conduct a comparative assessment between the living conditions the applicant would face upon return and those enjoyed in Italy, giving due weight to the social, employment, emotional, and educational integration built over the years of stay. The ruling held that special protection also safeguards personal dignity within the sphere of private life, and not merely physical integrity.

Cass. SS.UU. n. 24413/2021
Art. 32 par. 3 D.Lgs. 25/2008 — The Commission route

The grant of special protection during the assessment of the international protection application

Art. 32, paragraph 3, of D.Lgs. 28 January 2008, n. 25 (transposing the Asylum Procedures Directive 2013/32/EU) provides that when the Territorial Commission does not grant refugee status or subsidiary protection, but finds that the requirements for granting special protection under art. 19 TUI may be met, it transmits the case files to the Police Commissioner (Questore) for the issuance of a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) for special protection. This provision is fundamental because it allows a rejection of international protection to be converted into the grant of special protection, preventing the applicant's expulsion. An applicant who receives a rejection of their international protection application must therefore ascertain whether the Commission made this referral to the Questore and, if not, may challenge this aspect of the decision in court as well.

Law Decree D.L. 20/2023 (Cutro Decree) and resistant case law. The Cutro Decree attempted to restrict special protection by eliminating the autonomous reference to social ties and integration. Nonetheless, the vast majority of Italian courts (Tribunali) have continued to grant protection based on art. 8 ECHR (CEDU), holding that the restrictive domestic rule is incompatible with Convention obligations. The Court of Rome (Tribunale di Roma, ord. 5 aprile 2024) has raised a question of constitutional legitimacy. The Naples Court of Appeal (Corte d’Appello di Napoli) has confirmed on multiple occasions the primacy of art. 8 ECHR (CEDU) over domestic legislation. The legal framework is rapidly evolving and requires an up-to-date and thorough defence.

Which form of protection applies to your case

The forms of special protection we handle

Special protection is not a monolithic institution: it is divided into multiple legal categories, each with distinct statutory prerequisites and burdens of proof. We identify the category most favourable to the specific case and build the most solid defence strategy.

Art. 19 para. 1 TUI

Non-refoulement: absolute prohibition of expulsion

The principle of non-refoulement imposes an absolute and non-derogable prohibition against returning a foreign national to a country where they face a risk of persecution, torture, or inhuman and degrading treatment. It is founded on art. 19 para. 1 of the Consolidated Immigration Act (TUI), art. 3 ECHR (CEDU), and the 1951 Geneva Convention. It does not require proof of individualised personal persecution: a real risk, even if not personal, of being subjected to treatment contrary to art. 3 ECHR (CEDU) is sufficient. The ECtHR (Grand Chamber, J.K. v. Sweden, 2016) clarified that the risk is assessed on the basis of general conditions in the destination country and the individual circumstances of the applicant.

Absolute prohibition — No exceptions permitted
Social ties and integration (radicamento sociale)

Social ties and integration into the territory

Social integration (radicamento sociale) consists of the network of ties — employment, personal, community, educational — that the foreign national has built in Italy over the years of residence. Prior to Law Decree D.L. 20/2023, it was expressly recognised by art. 19 para. 1.1 TUI as an autonomous ground for special protection. Following the Cutro Decree, the vast majority of trial courts (giurisprudenza di merito) continue to recognise it under the Convention pursuant to art. 8 ECHR (CEDU), taking the view that the expulsion of an individual who has built their life in Italy violates the right to respect for private life protected by the Convention. Relevant factors include: years of residence, employment contracts, children enrolled in school in Italy, integration into the local community.

Convention-based protection under art. 8 ECHR (CEDU) still recognised
Art. 8 ECHR (CEDU)

Private and family life: art. 8 ECHR (CEDU)

Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights guarantees the right to respect for private and family life, home, and correspondence. The Strasbourg Court has developed an extensive body of case law requiring States to strike a balance between the public interest in the expulsion of an irregular foreign national and the private interest in preserving the family and private life established within the territory. The balancing criteria — the so-called “Boultif-Üner criteria” — include: the nature and seriousness of any criminal offence committed, duration of stay, family situation, the best interests of minor children, and obstacles to resettlement in the country of origin.

Constantly evolving ECHR (CEDU) jurisprudence — Continuous updating
Vulnerable individuals

Vulnerability: individual grounds for protection

The category of vulnerable individuals includes persons who require specific protection by reason of particular personal conditions: unaccompanied minors, victims of trafficking or exploitation, pregnant women or mothers with young children, persons with severe physical or mental disabilities, the elderly, victims of torture or violence. Vulnerability can independently justify the granting of special protection or substantiate an application for international protection. Legislative Decree D.Lgs. 142/2015 requires the authorities to promptly identify vulnerable persons within international protection proceedings.

Prompt assessment of the condition of vulnerability
Country of origin

Conditions in the country of origin: generalised risk

Even in the absence of direct individual persecution, the situation in the country of origin may justify the granting of special protection when there is a state of indiscriminate violence or systematic human rights violations such as to expose any individual to a real risk of suffering treatment contrary to art. 3 ECHR (CEDU). Country of Origin Information (COI) for the main countries of origin of protection applicants — Nigeria, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Gambia, Tunisia, Morocco, Senegal — is analysed with reference to the most up-to-date institutional sources (EASO, UNHCR, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International).

Up-to-date COI analysis across all principal countries
Victims of violence

Victims of domestic and gender-based violence

Women who are victims of gender-based violence or persecution on grounds of gender — forced marriage, female genital mutilation, domestic violence in countries that do not provide adequate protection — are entitled to international protection or, failing that, to special protection. Italian jurisprudence has recognised that women can constitute a “particular social group” under the Geneva Convention in relation to gender-based persecution. Alternatively, special protection safeguards a woman who, in the event of repatriation, would face a concrete risk of violence or discrimination that is not countered by the country of origin.

How we intervene

Defence strategies for special protection

Special protection requires a multi-level defence: from the collection of personal documentation to the analysis of country-of-origin conditions, from the interview before the Territorial Commission (Commissione territoriale) to the judicial appeal (ricorso). Every stage is decisive.

I.

Judicial appeal (ricorso) against the denial by the Commission

When the Territorial Commission for the Recognition of International Protection (Commissione territoriale per il riconoscimento della protezione internazionale) rejects the application without forwarding the file to the Police Commissioner (Questore) for an assessment of special protection, or when the Police Commissioner rejects the application for a special protection residence permit (permesso di soggiorno), the applicant may lodge an appeal (ricorso) before the immigration division of the Court (Tribunale). The appeal must be filed within 30 days of notification of the decision. The Court re-examines the merits of the protection claim, assessing all grounds of protection, including those not adequately considered by the Commission. The suspensive effect of the appeal prevents the expulsion of the applicant pending the proceedings.

II.

Preparation of the documentary file demonstrating social integration

For protection based on social integration (radicamento sociale), documentary evidence is decisive: employment contracts, pay slips, rent receipts, school enrolment records of children, active membership in associations, volunteer certificates, declarations from employers and neighbours, certificates of attendance in Italian language courses. Our firm assists the client in the systematic gathering and organisation of this evidentiary material, presenting it to the Court (Tribunale) in a structured and persuasive manner. A well-constructed documentary file can make the difference between admission and dismissal.

III.

COI analysis and evidence concerning the situation in the country

Country of Origin Information (COI) represents the backbone of the defence in international and special protection proceedings. Our firm constantly updates its database of institutional sources (EASO/EUAA, UNHCR, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, U.S. Department of State) for each country of origin of the applicants. We produce up-to-date reports in court concerning conditions in the country, the effectiveness of protection offered by local authorities, prison conditions, the treatment of minorities, and the situation of women. Up-to-date and well-targeted COI significantly reinforces the appeal (ricorso).

IV.

Convention-based protection under art. 8 ECHR (CEDU) after the Cutro Decree

Law Decree (D.L.) 20/2023 downsized protection based on social integration under Art. 19 of the Consolidated Immigration Act (TUI), but did not amend Art. 8 ECHR, which remains directly applicable in the Italian legal system pursuant to Art. 117 of the Italian Constitution. Our firm has developed a defense strategy relying directly on the treaty provision, citing the case law of the Strasbourg Court on the Boultif-Uner criteria and Italian trial and appellate court case law that continues to grant protection pursuant to Art. 8 ECHR even under the new regulatory framework. This approach has yielded good results in the case law of the Courts of Naples, Rome, and Milan.

V.

Preparation for the interview before the Territorial Commission

The interview before the Territorial Commission (Commissione territoriale) is the most critical stage of the entire international protection procedure: the statements made on that occasion will be weighed or challenged at every subsequent stage. Our firm prepares the client for the interview through in-depth preparatory meetings, analysis of the coherence of the account, identification of the most relevant elements of the personal history, and preparation for handling the most difficult questions. The lawyer's presence at the interview is a right recognized by law and is systematically exercised by our firm.

VI.

Urgent interim measure and stay of deportation

When the applicant faces imminent risk of deportation pending proceedings or an appeal, an urgent interim measure is the instrument preventing forced return before the court rules on the merits. The specialized Court may issue an urgent interim measure inaudita altera parte within very short timeframes — even within hours of lodging the appeal (ricorso) — ordering the suspension of the deportation order (decreto di espulsione). Our experience in urgent proceedings allows for immediate intervention even in the most critical situations, including cases where the applicant is already held in a Pre-removal Detention Centre (Centro di Permanenza per il Rimpatrio - CPR).

Special protection after the Cutro Decree: the updated framework as of March 2026. Law Decree (D.L.) 20/2023 eliminated the direct conversion of the special protection residence permit into a work residence permit, making the stabilization of residence more difficult. However, Italian Courts — with a prevailing trend — continue to recognize protection based on Art. 8 ECHR. The Constitutional Court may be called upon to rule on the compatibility of the new Art. 19, paragraph 1.1 TUI with the Constitution. Our firm constantly monitors the evolution of the legislative and jurisprudential framework and updates defense strategies in real time.

Case no. 1

Special protection on grounds of social integration: 12 years in Italy, expulsion averted — Tribunale di Napoli

Tribunale di Napoli, Sezione Specializzata Immigrazione — 2024 — Art. 19 co. 1.1 TUI, Art. 8 ECHR

A 38-year-old Moroccan national, residing in Italy for twelve years with residence permits (permessi di soggiorno) periodically renewed for work, had his application for international protection rejected by the Territorial Commission of Naples, an application submitted following the expiry of his work residence permit during the Covid-19 pandemic. The applicant had been in Italy since 2012: he had worked for seven years as a construction worker under a regular contract, paid social security contributions, obtained an Italian driving licence, spoke fluent Italian, attended vocational training courses recognised by Regione Campania, and was integrated into his parish community. He had had no actual ties to Morocco for many years: his parents were deceased and he no longer had a home in his country of origin. The Commission rejected the application, finding that the requirements for international protection were not met, without forwarding the case file to the Police Commissioner (Questore) for special protection (protezione speciale).

Our law firm filed an appeal (ricorso) with the specialized section of the Tribunale di Napoli, submitting a documentary file of 47 records evidencing the applicant's social and employment integration: employment contracts, payslips, tax returns, course attendance certificates, a statement from the parish priest of the community, statements from neighbours and colleagues, and documentation proving the absence of relatives in Morocco. The appeal grounded the request for special protection on Art. 8 ECHR, citing the Boultif-Uner criteria and judgment no. 24413/2021 of the Joint Sections of the Court of Cassation (Sezioni Unite). We demonstrated that expulsion would deprive the applicant of his entire private life built over twelve years of lawful residence in Italy, while in his country of origin there was no support network or realistic prospect of reintegration. The Court upheld the appeal by decree of 3 September 2024, granting special protection pursuant to Art. 8 ECHR and ordering the Police Commissioner (Questore) to issue the residence permit for special protection (permesso di soggiorno per protezione speciale). The permit was issued within thirty days of the notification of the decree.

Special protection granted — Residence permit issued — Expulsion permanently averted
Case no. 2

Medical non-refoulement: repatriation to Nigeria with severe psychiatric condition — Art. 3 ECHR

Tribunale di Napoli, Sezione Specializzata Immigrazione — 2023 — Art. 19 co. 1 TUI, Art. 3 ECHR

A 29-year-old Nigerian national, who had been an applicant for international protection for three years, had her application for protection rejected by the Territorial Commission (Commissione Territoriale), despite receiving care from the psychiatric services of the Local Health Authority of Naples (Distretto Sanitario di Napoli) for a severe post-traumatic stress disorder resulting from episodes of violence suffered during the migration journey. Her treating psychiatrist had certified that the disruption of the ongoing pharmacological and therapeutic treatment, caused by repatriation to Nigeria, would entail a concrete risk of acute relapse with the possibility of self-harm. In Nigeria, access to psychiatric care is extremely limited in the applicant's rural areas of origin, as documented by available COI.

Our firm filed an appeal (ricorso) basing it primarily on Art. 3 ECHR in the dimension of medical non-refoulement recognised by the Grand Chamber in the judgment Paposhvili v. Belgium of 13 December 2016: when the repatriation of a person with a serious illness entails the risk of a rapid and irreversible deterioration of their state of health such as to cause intense suffering or accelerated death, a violation of Art. 3 ECHR is established. We produced detailed medical documentation: a report from the supervising psychiatrist, medical records from the Local Health District (Distretto Sanitario), a report from the cultural mediator who assisted the applicant, and updated COI on the accessibility of psychiatric care in Nigeria. The Court granted the appeal by decree of 14 November 2023, recognising special protection (protezione speciale) and ordering the issuance of the residence permit for special protection (permesso per protezione speciale). The applicant was able to continue her therapeutic treatment in Italy.

Medical non-refoulement recognised — Special protection granted — Treatment continued
Case no. 3

Special protection for family life: father of an Italian child — Tribunale di Napoli

Tribunale di Napoli, Specialized Immigration Section — 2024 — Art. 19 para. 1.1 TUI, Art. 8 ECHR, Art. 3 UN Convention on the Rights of the Child

A 31-year-old Gambian citizen, an applicant for international protection whose application had been rejected by the Commission (Commissione Territoriale), was the legally recognised father of a three-year-old child born in Italy from a relationship with an Italian citizen. The separation from his partner had complicated the relationship with his son, but the young man regularly took part in the child’s upbringing: he picked him up from school three times a week, spent weekends with him, and contributed financially to his support. The child had Italian citizenship by birth, attended nursery school in Naples, and had built his life entirely in Italy. The father’s repatriation to The Gambia would have deprived the child of a paternal figure who was present and involved in his growth.

We filed an appeal (ricorso) before the Tribunale di Napoli basing the defence on three pillars: Art. 8 ECHR in the dimension of the applicant’s right to family life, Art. 3 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (best interests of the child), and the settled case law of the Strasbourg Court according to which the expulsion of a parent of a minor child residing in the expelling country requires particularly serious reasons to pass the proportionality test under Art. 8 ECHR. We submitted: the ruling of the Juvenile Court (Tribunale per i Minorenni) certifying the father’s visitation rights, an extract of the child’s birth certificate showing paternal recognition, a statement from the mother on the father’s presence in the child’s life, receipts of bank transfers sent for the child’s maintenance, and photographs documenting the emotional bond. The Court granted special protection (protezione speciale) by decree of 7 March 2024, stating that the best interests of the minor Italian child required that he not be deprived of his father’s presence. The residence permit for special protection (permesso per protezione speciale) was issued within thirty days.

Special protection recognised — Best interests of the child safeguarded — Family preserved
Case no. 4

Woman victim of trafficking and gender-based violence in Nigeria — Special protection and Art. 3 ECHR

Corte d’Appello di Napoli, Immigration Section — 2023 — Art. 19 TUI, Art. 3 ECHR, Art. 1A Geneva Convention

A 26-year-old Nigerian woman had arrived in Italy via the Libyan routes after falling victim to human trafficking for the purpose of sexual exploitation. Her journey had been organised by a criminal network that had taken advantage of her condition of extreme poverty, promising her employment in Europe. After receiving assistance from an anti-trafficking organisation and reporting her exploiters, she had lodged an application for international protection. The Commission (Commissione Territoriale) had acknowledged the existence of indicators of trafficking, but had rejected the application for international protection on the grounds that Nigeria provided sufficient protection to trafficking victims. The Court of first instance had upheld the rejection.

Our firm lodged an appeal (appello) before the Corte d’Appello di Napoli, focusing the challenge on three aspects: first, the risk of retaliation by the criminal network in the event of repatriation, documented through COIs on Nigerian organised crime active in human trafficking; second, the ineffectiveness of the protection afforded by the Nigerian State to trafficking victims, attested by reports from UNHCR and Human Rights Watch; third, the violation of Art. 3 ECHR due to the concrete risk of falling back into the hands of the exploiters. The Court of Appeal upheld the appeal by decree of 22 May 2023, granting the applicant subsidiary protection (protezione sussidiaria) pursuant to Art. 14(b) of Legislative Decree no. 251/2007 as a primary claim, and special protection (protezione speciale) in the alternative. The applicant obtained her residence permit (titolo di soggiorno) and embarked on a path of vocational integration with the support of the anti-trafficking organisation assisting her.

Subsidiary protection granted on appeal — Woman protected from the risk of re-trafficking
Case no. 5

Special protection post-Cutro Decree: Art. 8 ECHR directly applied — Tribunale di Napoli 2025

Tribunale di Napoli, Specialized Immigration Section — 2025 — Art. 8 ECHR, Art. 117 Const., D.L. 20/2023

A 35-year-old Bangladeshi citizen, who had resided in Italy for eight years holding a residence permit for employment (permesso di soggiorno per lavoro) renewed multiple times, had applied for special protection (protezione speciale) after the permit expired during a period of unemployment. The application had been submitted in 2024, following the entry into force of Law Decree no. 20/2023 (Cutro Decree), which had amended Art. 19 para. 1.1 TUI. The Police Commissioner (Questore) had rejected the application for special protection, considering that, under the new legislative framework, social integration no longer constituted an independent ground for protection, and that the applicant did not satisfy the more stringent requirements of the reworded provision. The applicant had eight years of life built in Italy: an open-ended employment contract (later suspended due to a company crisis), active participation in a Bangladeshi cultural association in Naples, and enrollment in a bachelor’s degree programme in Economics and Commerce.

Our firm filed an appeal (ricorso) before the Tribunale di Napoli, basing the defence exclusively on Art. 8 ECHR as a provision directly applicable within the Italian legal system pursuant to Art. 117 para. 1 of the Constitution, arguing that the amendment made by Law Decree no. 20/2023 to Art. 19 para. 1.1 TUI did not extinguish the conventional right to protection of private life, which operates in the Italian legal order autonomously and hierarchically above domestic law. The appeal cited extensive lower court jurisprudence that, even after the Cutro Decree, continued to grant protection under Art. 8 ECHR (Trib. Roma, 12 January 2024; Trib. Milano, 5 March 2024; Trib. Firenze, 9 April 2024), as well as court orders through which several courts had raised questions of constitutional legitimacy regarding the new provision. The Tribunale di Napoli upheld the appeal by decree of 18 February 2025, directly applying Art. 8 ECHR and recognising special protection founded on the private life built over eight years of integration in Italy. The decree established an important precedent in post-Cutro Decree Neapolitan jurisprudence.

Art. 8 ECHR applied directly — Special protection recognised post Law Decree no. 20/2023
