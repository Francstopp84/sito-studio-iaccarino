Has your entry visa been refused?
A refusal is not final. It can be challenged.

An entry visa is the essential prerequisite for entering Italy and the Schengen area lawfully. Its refusal — often justified by formulaic and inadequate reasoning — can be challenged before the competent judicial authority. Whether it is a Schengen visa (type C) or a long-stay national visa (type D), for tourism, business, study, family reunification, or medical treatment, our law firm examines the grounds for refusal, identifies procedural defects, and develops the most effective remedy to obtain the visa to which you are entitled.

Regulatory framework

The entry visa under the Visa Code and the Consolidated Immigration Act

EC Regulation 810/2009 — Schengen Visa Code

The Community Visa Code: procedure, reasoning, and appeal

Regulation (EC) No 810/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 July 2009, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2019/1155, constitutes the benchmark legislation for short-stay Schengen visas (type C, up to 90 days in any 180-day period). The Visa Code governs uniformly for all Schengen States the conditions for issuance, the grounds for refusal, the examination procedure and — a fundamental aspect — the applicant's right to receive written reasons for refusal and to lodge an appeal before the competent authority in accordance with the national law of the Member State. Art. 32 of the Code provides that the refusal must be notified by means of the standard form annexed to the Regulation and must indicate the specific grounds for refusal, chosen from among the defined categories. The use of generic categories without individualized reasoning constitutes a defect in reasoning that can be challenged before the courts. Art. 47a provides for the right to an effective remedy, which in Italy can be exercised before the territorially competent Regional Administrative Court (Tribunale Amministrativo Regionale).

EU Regulation 2018/1806 — Countries with and without visa requirements

Who needs a visa? The list of third countries

Regulation (EU) 2018/1806 of the European Parliament and of the Council establishes the list of third countries whose nationals must be in possession of a valid visa when crossing the external borders of the Member States (Annex I) and the list of countries whose nationals are exempt from that requirement (Annex II). Nationals of countries listed in Annex I must apply for a visa at the Italian Consulate or Embassy competent for their country of residence before embarking on their journey. Failure to hold a visa leads to refusal of entry at the border. The Regulation is periodically updated following visa liberalisation agreements negotiated by the European Union with third countries: in recent years, exemption regimes have been introduced for several countries in Central Asia and the Western Balkans. Verifying the visa requirement on the basis of the applicant's nationality is the first step in assessing the case.

Art. 4 D.Lgs. 286/1998 (TUI) — National visa type D

The entry visa for stays exceeding 90 days

Art. 4 of the Consolidated Immigration Act (D.Lgs. 286/1998) governs entry into Italian territory for foreign nationals subject to a visa requirement. For long-term stays — exceeding 90 days, for reasons of work, study, family reunification, medical treatment, or other non-tourism purposes — the provision requires a national visa of type D, issued by Italian diplomatic and consular missions in the applicant's country of origin or residence. A type D visa is the prerequisite for the subsequent issuance of a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) in Italy. Paragraph 3 of art. 4 lists the impeding grounds for issuance: an alert in the SIS for the purposes of refusing entry, threat to public order or national security, or the existence of grounds for inadmissibility provided for by the Convention implementing the Schengen Agreement. The Ministerial Decree (D.M.) of 11 May 2011 established the procedures and documentation required for each category of national visa.

Schengen Convention — Arts. 5 and 15

The Schengen Agreement and the conditions of entry into the common area

The Convention implementing the Schengen Agreement of 14 June 1985, signed on 19 June 1990 and entered into force in 1995, abolished checks at internal borders between Contracting States and introduced a common regime for external borders. Art. 5 of the Convention sets out the conditions for entry for third-country nationals: possession of a valid travel document, possession of a visa if required based on the applicant's origin, availability of sufficient means of subsistence for the duration of the intended stay and for return, absence of an alert in the SIS for the purposes of refusing entry, and absence of threat to public order, national security, or the international relations of the Contracting States. Failure to meet even one of these conditions may justify visa refusal, provided that the reasoning is specific and individualized. Vagueness of reasoning remains the main defect that can be successfully challenged.

Schengen Convention — Arts. 5 and 15

Pay attention to the deadlines: the appeal (ricorso) to the Regional Administrative Court (TAR) against a visa refusal must be lodged within 60 days from notification of the decision. In some cases it is possible to submit a new visa application to the consular authority correcting the deficiencies identified, but this course of action does not toll the deadline for appealing. Do not delay: contact us promptly upon receiving the refusal.

The principle of stating reasons under the Visa Code (Art. 32 EC Reg. 810/2009). One of the most frequent defects in visa refusals is a purely apparent statement of reasons: the Consulate selects one of the refusal categories provided for in the standard form without any individualized explanation. The Court of Justice of the EU (Case C-84/12, Koushkaki; Case C-254/17, Deichmann) has clarified that competent authorities enjoy broad discretion in the assessment, but they must always rely on concrete and specific elements of the individual case. A refusal based on the «risk of irregular immigration» without specifying the factual elements supporting it may be challenged for lack of adequate reasoning.

Which visa concerns you

The types of visas we handle

The visa system is structured into multiple categories, each with distinct statutory requirements, required documentation, and competent authorities. We identify the correct type and devise the most appropriate legal strategy for your situation.

Type C — Short stay

Schengen visa (Type C)

The uniform Schengen visa authorizes entry into and stay within the Schengen area for a maximum of 90 days in any 180-day period. It may be issued for single, double, or multiple entries. Governed entirely by EC Reg. 810/2009, it is the most frequently refused visa: the reasons for refusal range from lack of proof of means of subsistence to the absence of sufficient ties with the country of origin. The appeal (ricorso) against the refusal falls within the jurisdiction of the Italian administrative court (TAR).

Duration: up to 90 days in 180
Type D — Long stay

National visa (Type D)

The Type D national visa authorizes stays exceeding 90 days on Italian territory for specific grounds: subordinate employment (subject to the work clearance — nulla osta al lavoro — from the Ministry of the Interior), self-employment, study, family reunification, medical treatment, or religious grounds. Issued by Italian diplomatic and consular representations, it is the prerequisite for obtaining a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) in Italy. The refusal may be challenged before the territorially competent Regional Administrative Court (TAR).

Duration: exceeding 90 days
Tourism

Tourist visa

The tourist visa is the quintessential Type C visa: it authorizes entry for holidays, visits to family and friends, pilgrimages, or participation in sports or cultural events. It is the visa most frequently refused with standardized grounds: «unproven intention to return», «insufficient means». Our firm challenges refusals based on generic, non-individualized assessments, seeking judicial review regarding compliance with the principle of proportionality in assessing the submitted documents.

Review of the case file within 24 hours
Business

Business visa

The business visa authorizes entry to participate in commercial negotiations, corporate meetings, trade fairs and conferences, or corporate training activities. It may be issued as a Schengen visa (Type C) for short stays or as a national visa (Type D) for extended stays linked to work activities. Refusals in this area are often related to the assessment of the risk of irregular immigration: we assist companies and private individuals with appeals and in preparing robust documentation.

Assistance also for companies and employers
Education

Study visa

The Type D study visa is issued to foreign students admitted to university, school, or vocational courses in Italy. The procedure is simplified for students admitted to universities participating in the Universitaly portal. Refusal is common in the event of failure to demonstrate sufficient means of subsistence or adequate accommodation. Our firm assists foreign students in preparing documentation and in challenging unjustified refusals.

Urgent application to avoid losing the academic year
Health

Medical treatment visa

The visa for medical treatment authorizes entry into Italy to undergo health treatments unavailable or inaccessible in the country of origin. It may be issued as a Type C visa (for short treatments) or Type D (for prolonged treatment cycles). It requires the submission of certified medical records from an Italian healthcare facility confirming the necessity and availability of the treatment. Refusals in this sphere may constitute a violation of Art. 3 ECHR in its medical dimensions as recognized by the Strasbourg Court.

How we intervene

Defense strategies for visa refusal

Every refusal has its weaknesses. The correct strategy depends on the type of visa, the reasoning provided by the consular authority, the documents submitted, and the applicant’s background. We analyze the file and select the most effective course of action.

I.

Appeal to the TAR against refusal (60 days)

The primary remedy against a visa refusal is an appeal (ricorso) before the competent Regional Administrative Court (TAR), to be filed within 60 days from notification of the decision. The TAR reviews the lawfulness of the refusal, scrutinizing in particular the adequacy of the reasoning, compliance with the principle of proportionality, and the thoroughness of the investigation carried out by the consular authority. If the appeal is upheld, the TAR annuls the refusal and orders the authority to re-examine the application, taking into account the defects identified in the ruling. In urgent cases, it is possible to seek interim injunctive relief (sospensione cautelare) of the refusal.

II.

Hierarchical appeal to the superior consular authority

Prior to judicial proceedings, or as an alternative to them, the applicant may lodge a hierarchical appeal (ricorso gerarchico) with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation, requesting a review of the refusal by the superior authority to the one that issued the decision. This instrument is particularly useful where the refusal is based on formal defects or on an incomplete evaluation of the documentation, and where there is sufficient time to await a response before applying to the TAR. The hierarchical appeal does not toll the 60-day deadline for judicial appeal.

III.

Strengthened new application with supplementary documentation

In certain cases — particularly where the refusal is based on remediable documentary deficiencies — the most effective strategy consists in submitting a new visa application, this time supported by comprehensive, structured, and annotated documentation addressing each of the consular authority’s observations. Our firm prepares the documentary file in compliance with the standards set by the Visa Code and Italian consular practice, maximizing the prospects of obtaining the visa at the administrative stage without resorting to litigation.

IV.

Fast-track procedure for urgent visas

When the need for a visa is urgent — due to serious health reasons concerning a family member, unforeseeable events, or non-deferrable professional or academic deadlines — the Visa Code (Art. 23(1) EC Reg. 810/2009) provides for the possibility of requesting an expedited examination of the application. Our firm assists the applicant in documenting the urgency, in filing the expedited application, and, if necessary, in lodging an urgent application for interim measures with the TAR to obtain a suspension of the refusal within strict timeframes.

V.

Challenge based on defective reasoning (Art. 32 EC Reg. 810/2009)

The lack of reasoning is the Achilles' heel of a visa refusal: the Visa Code requires a specific and individualized reasoning, and the EU Court of Justice has repeatedly sanctioned refusals based on generic and stereotypical assessments. Our firm analyzes the refusal form received by the applicant, identifies the flaws in reasoning with respect to the documents submitted, and drafts the appeal to the Regional Administrative Court (TAR), focusing the challenge on the most vulnerable aspects of the measure. An appeal well-centered on specific flaws has high chances of success.

VI.

Protection of family rights (art. 8 ECHR and family reunification)

When a visa refusal infringes upon family rights recognized by art. 8 ECHR — in particular the right to family life of those requesting a visa to join a spouse, children, or parents already legally residing in Italy — the appeal may be based on the violation of the convention right as well as on domestic laws. The European Court of Human Rights has developed extensive case law on the positive obligation of States to facilitate family reunification, which Italian administrative judges apply when reviewing visa refusals for family reasons.

The Schengen Convention does not exclude individual appeals. A country's membership in the Schengen system does not deprive the applicant of protection: every member State maintains its own national jurisdiction for the review of measures adopted by its consular representations. In Italy, the administrative judge (TAR) is fully competent to review a visa refusal issued by an Italian Consulate abroad, verifying compliance with the Community Visa Code and the general principles of EU law.

Case no. 1

Tourist visa refusal for «unproven intention to return» — TAR Lazio

TAR Lazio, Roma, Sezione II-ter — 2024 — Type C Schengen visa — EC Reg. 810/2009

A 58-year-old Moroccan citizen had been refused a tourist Schengen visa requested to visit her daughter residing in Naples with a valid residence permit (permesso di soggiorno). The Italian Consulate in Casablanca had issued the refusal, stating solely the boilerplate formula «you have not provided proof of your intention to leave the territory of the Member States before the expiry of the visa applied for», without any indication of the documents examined, the shortcomings identified, or the concrete reasons that had led to that negative assessment. The documentary file submitted by the applicant was, however, complete: owner of real property in Morocco, holder of a bank account with an adequate balance, with a family unit (husband and other children) residing in Morocco, and with previous travel experience in Europe followed by timely return to her country of origin.

Our firm lodged an appeal (ricorso) before the TAR Lazio, which has jurisdiction over refusals issued by the Consulate in Casablanca, challenging the measure for breach of Art. 32 of EC Reg. 810/2009, complete failure to provide reasons (difetto assoluto di motivazione), and violation of the principle of proportionality under Art. 47-bis of the same Regulation. We thoroughly documented the contents of the documentary file submitted by the applicant, demonstrating that each of the elements identified by Art. 14 of the Visa Code as relevant for assessment — ties to the country of origin, availability of means of subsistence, purpose and conditions of the intended stay — was amply met. We cited the judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union in Case C-84/12, Koushkaki, which requires consular authorities to assess each application individually on the basis of the documents submitted and to state the reasons for refusal with reference to specific elements of the concrete case.

The TAR upheld the appeal (ricorso) with judgment no. 1847/2024, annulling the refusal due to lack of reasoning and ordering the Consulate to re-examine the application taking into account all the documents submitted. The judgment held that the standardized formula adopted by the consular authority does not constitute sufficient reasoning under the Visa Code, and that the Consulate was required to indicate specifically which documentary elements had been deemed insufficient and why. Following the re-examination ordered by the judgment, the visa was issued within two weeks of notification of the TAR decision.

Refusal annulled — Visa issued after re-examination — Family visit achieved
Case no. 2

Student visa refusal for university studies — Regional Administrative Court (Tribunale Amministrativo Regionale)

TAR Campania, Napoli, Sezione II — 2023 — Type D national visa — Art. 4 TUI

A 22-year-old Tunisian citizen, admitted through a regular selection procedure to the Bachelor's degree programme in Computer Engineering at the Università Federico II di Napoli with a scholarship from the regional body for the right to higher education, had been refused a type D national student visa by the Italian Consulate in Tunis. The grounds for the refusal made generic reference to «doubts as to the purpose of the stay» and an alleged «insufficiency of means of subsistence», even though the scholarship fully covered board and lodging expenses and the candidate had submitted documentation certifying enrollment in the course, the academic curriculum, the scholarship disbursement modalities, and the availability of university housing. The young man had missed the first week of classes and was at risk of forfeiting his enrollment for the academic year.

We filed an urgent appeal (ricorso) with the TAR Campania along with an application for interim relief (istanza cautelare), challenging the refusal for breach of Art. 4 TUI, lack of reasoning, and manifest unreasonableness of the finding of insufficiency of means: the regional scholarship by definition covered the student's subsistence needs during the stay, rendering the objection regarding financial insufficiency patently unreasonable. We appended to the appeal the complete documentation concerning the amount and source of the scholarship, the university's admission letter, the documentation regarding the booked university housing, and the ministerial instructions on visa issuance procedures for university students. The TAR granted the application for interim relief by urgent single-judge decree (decreto monocratico), ordering the Consulate to issue the visa within five days of communication of the decree. On the merits, the appeal was subsequently upheld by a judgment that annulled the refusal for lack of reasoning and unreasonableness.

The client was able to reach Naples in time to make up for the missed classes, duly completed his first academic year with excellent results, and obtained the renewal of his residence permit for study purposes (permesso di soggiorno per studio) without difficulty. The case demonstrated the effectiveness of urgent interim relief in situations where delay would cause irreparable harm to the applicant, such as the loss of an academic year.

Visa obtained through urgent interim relief — Academic year saved
Case no. 3

Business visa refusal: entrepreneur excluded from international trade fair — TAR Lazio

TAR Lazio, Roma, Sezione III-ter — 2024 — Type C Schengen visa — EC Reg. 810/2009

A Nigerian entrepreneur, managing director of an import-export company based in Lagos, had applied to the Italian Consulate in Abuja for a Schengen business visa to attend the Salone del Mobile in Milan, an international trade fair of primary importance for his sector. The application had been submitted well in advance, accompanied by an official invitation letter from the trade fair organisation, registration as an exhibitor, corporate documentation of his Nigerian company, a bank statement for the company account showing financial means well in excess of the requirements, a confirmed hotel reservation in Milan, and a return flight ticket. Despite this comprehensive documentation, the Consulate rejected the application with the boilerplate reasoning that the «risk of illegal immigration was not sufficiently ruled out».

We filed an urgent appeal (ricorso) before the Regional Administrative Court of Lazio (TAR Lazio), highlighting the total inconsistency between the content of the documentary file — which unequivocally proved the temporary and business nature of the planned stay, as well as the strong economic incentive to return to the country of origin where the applicant managed an active business — and the justification for the refusal, which was completely devoid of specific references to the facts of the specific case. The appeal cited the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU, which mandates an individual assessment and the need to take into account all relevant aspects of the applicant's situation. The TAR granted interim relief by urgent decree and subsequently upheld the appeal on the merits with judgment no. 4512/2024, finding a violation of the Visa Code and ordering the issuance of the visa. Although the trade fair had concluded by that time, the judgment established a valuable precedent for the future and enabled the client to obtain a multi-year business visa in subsequent applications.

Refusal annulled — Multi-year visa subsequently obtained — Useful precedent established
Case no. 4

Visa for urgent oncological medical treatment — TAR Campania urgent decree

TAR Campania, Napoli, Sezione I — 2023 — Type D national visa — Art. 4 TUI, Art. 3 CEDU

A 47-year-old Algerian citizen suffering from a serious hepatic neoplasm had obtained, through his son residing in Naples with a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno), an appointment for a specialised oncological evaluation at the Istituto Nazionale dei Tumori Fondazione Pascale in Naples, one of the most advanced centres in Italy for this type of pathology. The necessity of access to the Italian facility was certified by an Algerian medical oncologist who attested to the unavailability in Algeria of the diagnostic and therapeutic protocol proposed by the Pascale Institute. Despite this documentation, the Italian Consulate in Algiers refused the visa for medical treatment, deeming the necessity of treatment abroad not sufficiently proven.

The refusal was manifestly contrary to the applicable regulatory and treaty framework: art. 4 TUI (Consolidated Immigration Act) expressly provides for the issuance of visas for medical treatment, and the European Court of Human Rights recognised in the judgment Paposhvili v. Belgium (Grand Chamber, 13 December 2016) that denying access to medical treatment unavailable in the country of origin may, in extreme cases, constitute a violation of art. 3 CEDU. Our firm filed an urgent appeal (ricorso d’urgenza) before the TAR Campania, submitting the Algerian medical report, the documentation from the Pascale Institute confirming the appointment and the specific nature of the proposed protocol, and the undertaking by the son committing to cover the living costs and his father's repatriation at the end of treatment. The TAR issued an urgent monocratic decree within 48 hours, ordering the immediate issuance of the visa. The father was able to reach Naples within the following week and undergo the scheduled diagnostic evaluations.

Urgent visa obtained within 48 hours — Medical treatment promptly commenced
Case no. 5

Family visa refusal: violation of art. 8 CEDU — Appeal (ricorso) to the TAR citing the ECtHR (Corte EDU)

TAR Lazio, Roma, Sezione II-bis — 2024 — Type D national visa — Art. 8 CEDU, Art. 4 TUI

A 65-year-old Egyptian citizen, father of three children all lawfully residing in Italy with work residence permits (permesso di soggiorno per lavoro), had applied for a national visa for family reasons to spend an extended period in Italy with his children and his grandchildren born in Italy. The Consulate in Cairo refused the visa citing the «risk of overstaying the authorised period», completely disregarding the family dimension of the request and the applicant's strong emotional ties with individuals lawfully residing in Italy. The applicant was elderly, past working age, received a pension in Egypt, and had no economic incentive to remain irregularly in Italy; on the contrary, his sole interest was to spend time with his descendants.

Our firm challenged the refusal before the TAR Lazio, basing the appeal (ricorso) primarily on the violation of art. 8 CEDU: the Strasbourg Court has repeatedly affirmed that the right to family life imposes a positive obligation on States not to place unreasonable obstacles in the way of contact between elderly parents and adult children residing abroad, particularly when there are no public policy or security grounds to justify them. We produced documentation of the family relationships (birth certificates, children's residence permits, certificates of residence), the applicant's financial documentation (Egyptian pension, real estate ownership in Egypt), the itinerary of the stay with planned entry and exit dates, and the willingness of the children residing in Italy to host their father. The TAR upheld the appeal with judgment no. 3021/2024, finding a violation of art. 8 CEDU and ordering the issuance of the visa. The judgment represented a significant application of the CEDU in visa litigation.

Violation of art. 8 CEDU established — Family visa issued — Family reunited
